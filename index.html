Abstract

Robust robotic perception in dynamic human spaces requires distinguishing physical geometric changes from mere appearance variations. When a robot revisits a scene and its map or perception model fails, the cause may be geometric — objects were moved, added, or removed — or appearance-based — the same geometry looks different under changed lighting. Existing indoor datasets either hold the scene static, conflate the two sources of change, or lack independent reference geometry to verify what actually changed.

We present SceneGraphDataset, an indoor RGB-D dataset designed to strictly disentangle layout modifications from illumination shifts. Each of 20 diverse real-world scenes is captured in a core 2 × 2 factorial design — two physical layout states observed under artificial and natural light, so that any two sequences in the same row or column differ in exactly one factor — supplemented by a third, high-clutter state captured under natural light only. Every state is grounded in a terrestrial laser scan, and every RGB frame carries a manually labelled and verified instance mask whose identity persists across frames, sequences, and states. We introduce three benchmarks on top of the data — Scene Change Detection, Open-Vocabulary Video Instance Segmentation, and Embodied Question Answering — and show that state-of-the-art models still hallucinate changes under illumination shifts, lose track of small and rare objects, and struggle to reason about how a scene evolved.

Three things you cannot get elsewhere

Layout and light as independent axes

state 1 · nat state 2 · nat

Static benchmarks (ScanNet, ScanNet++) never revisit a scene; revisit datasets (3RScan) leave lighting uncontrolled. Here each factor can be varied on its own, so a failure is attributed to geometry or to appearance instead of being left ambiguous.

Artifact-free 2D masks with persistent IDs

Masks are annotated on the images and verified frame by frame — not projected from a labelled mesh with its boundary artifacts. The same object keeps the same ID in every frame, sequence, and state, in 2D and in 3D.

A survey-grade reference for every state

Laser-scanned datasets (ScanNet++, ARKitScenes) give one static snapshot. Here every state has its own terrestrial scan, so object-level changes are measured rather than inferred from a depth reconstruction — and reference clouds, change lists, and camera poses of all sequences live in one metric coordinate frame.

The lighting gap is real and it varies

Press and hold a tile (or hover) to switch from artificial to natural light. Some rooms barely change; some flip completely. Windowless rooms get the natural condition emulated with auxiliary lamps.

States and illumination

A state is a physical configuration of the scene — which objects are present and where they are, as captured in that state's laser scan. States are cumulative: the core states 1 and 2 are recorded under both illumination conditions, state 3 under natural light only.

Three benchmarks, one perception-to-reasoning pipeline

From detecting that something changed, to tracking every object through time, to answering questions about how the scene evolved. All baselines run out of the box with official weights — no fine-tuning on our data.

1 Scene Change Detection

Given a reference and a target image from the same camera pose, predict the pixels that changed — added, removed, or relocated objects. Reference views are rendered from a 3D Gaussian Splatting model of state1_art at the exact poses of the target sequence, so pairs are perfectly aligned; ground truth unites verified 2D masks of the target state with 3D projections of former object locations.

600 image pairs · 30 per scene · real targets under both lighting conditions, plus no-change control pairs.

Lighting alone fools every method. On state1_nat nothing physically changed, yet specificity drops for all three methods compared with state1_art — they hallucinate geometric change from appearance. GeSCF is the most accurate overall; GeoSCD recalls the most but over-predicts at ~17 s per pair; RSCD is fastest (0.37 s) but least precise.

2 Open-Vocabulary Video Instance Segmentation

Detect, segment, and track every object through a sequence with no fixed label set, keeping identities through occlusion and appearance change. Built on six locations, 30-second clips at 5 fps, with labels split by linguistic frequency (common vs. uncommon head nouns) and by 3D size (small < 0.1 m³, medium, large ≥ 1 m³).

Metrics follow BURST: HOTA (detection × association) and Track mAP.

HOTA by label split, averaged over the six locations

Small and rare objects are where tracking breaks. SAM3 leads by a wide margin (HOTA 18.2 vs. < 3 for GLEE and Ov2Seg) at 2.9 s per frame, yet loses more than half of its score from large to small objects (32.5 → 13.4) and drops sharply on uncommon classes. Cluttered locations push the lighter models to near zero.

3 Embodied Question Answering

Answer natural-language questions about one state or an ordered pair of states, returning a canonical answer and, where relevant, the persistent instance IDs. 1,874 questions over all 20 scenes in seven categories — existence, counting, comparison, localization, appearance, disappearance, movement — and three difficulty levels, proposed by an LLM from the verified scene graph and checked by human annotators.

Two zero-shot tracks: an image-free LLM scene-graph track (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, textual graph + computed change lists) and a VLM track (InternVL3-38B, per-state labelled minimaps + textual graph + deterministic solvers). Scoring is exact-match with no partial credit.

Accuracy by question category (%)

Existence is easy; localization and movement are not. Both tracks exceed 93 % on existence, but localization is the weakest category for Qwen (57.6 %) and movement for InternVL (27.2 %), even with perfectly verified scene annotations as input. Overall: 69.3 % (Qwen) and 52.7 % (InternVL); accuracy falls monotonically from easy to hard questions.
